# Symmetric & Skew Symmetric Matrix

### **Symmetric and Skew-Symmetric Matrices**

#### **1. Symmetric Matrix**

A **square matrix** $A$ is called **symmetric** if it is equal to its **transpose**, i.e.,

$$A^T = A \; \text{or } A'=A$$

This means that the elements of the matrix satisfy:

$$a_{ij} = a_{ji} \quad \text{for all } i, j$$

**Example of a Symmetric Matrix**

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 & 5 \\ 3 & 5 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$$

Here, $A^T = A$, so it is symmetric.

- $AA' \text{ is always symmetric}$
- $\frac{A+A'}{2} \; \text{is always symmetric}$

If $A$ and $B$ are symmetric:
- $A + B$ & $A-B$ are also symmetric
- But $AB$ & $BA$ may or may not be

> Transpose of an identity matrix is still an identity matrix.
#### **2. Skew-Symmetric Matrix**

A **square matrix** $A$ is called **skew-symmetric** if its **transpose is equal to its negative**, i.e.,

$$A^T = -A$$

This means that the elements of the matrix satisfy:

$$a_{ij} = -a_{ji} \quad \text{for all } i, j$$

Also, all **diagonal elements** of a skew-symmetric matrix must be **zero**, since:

$$a_{ii} = -a_{ii} \Rightarrow 2a_{ii} = 0 \Rightarrow a_{ii} = 0$$

**Example of a Skew-Symmetric Matrix**

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -2 & -3 \\ 2 & 0 & -5 \\ 3 & 5 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Here, $A^T = -A$, so it is skew-symmetric.

- $\frac{A-A'}{2} \; \text{is always skew symmetric}$
- $\frac{A+A'}{2}+\frac{A-A'}{2}=A$
- $a_{ij}=0 \; [ \;for\; i =j]$

---

### **Key Properties:**

1. **Every square matrix** can be expressed as the sum of a symmetric and a skew-symmetric matrix: $$A = \frac{A + A^T}{2} + \frac{A - A^T}{2}$$
2. **The product of two symmetric matrices is symmetric** if they commute, i.e., $AB = BA$.
3. **Skew-symmetric matrices have eigenvalues that are either 0 or purely imaginary numbers**.

---
### **Orthogonal Matrix**

A **square matrix** $A$ is called an **orthogonal matrix** if its **transpose** is equal to its **inverse**, i.e.,

$A^T A = A A^T = I$

where $I$ is the **identity matrix**.

### **Key Condition for Orthogonality**

$$A^T = A^{-1}$$

This means that multiplying the matrix by its transpose results in the identity matrix.

---

### **Example of an Orthogonal Matrix**

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \\ -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \end{bmatrix}$$

Let's verify:

$$A^T A = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \\ \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \\ -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = I$$

Since $A^T A = I$, $A$ is orthogonal.

---

### **Key Properties of Orthogonal Matrices**

1. **Preserves Vector Lengths**: If $A$ is orthogonal, then for any vector $x$, $\|Ax\| = \|x\|$
2. **Preserves Dot Product**: If $A$ is orthogonal, then for any vectors $u$ and $v$, $(Au) \cdot (Av) = u \cdot v$
3. **Determinant is Either +1 or -1**: $\det(A) = \pm 1$
4. **Columns and Rows Form an Orthonormal Basis**: The columns (and rows) of an orthogonal matrix are **orthonormal vectors**, meaning they are unit vectors and perpendicular to each other.
5. If $A$ & $B$ are orthogonal, then $AB$ & $BA$ are orthogonal.
6. If a matrix is an orthogonal matrix then $\|A\| = \pm 1$                     (*$\|A\| \rightarrow \text{Determinant of A}$*)
   but if $\|A\| = \pm 1$ then it may or may not be orthogonal.
