### Q1. Calculate $2^{100} \pmod 7$.

Solution:

We can use Fermat's Little Theorem, which states that if $p$ is a prime number, then for any integer $a$, $a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$.

1. Here, $a = 2$ and $p = 7$.
    
2. According to the theorem: $2^{7-1} \equiv 1 \pmod 7$, so $2^6 \equiv 1 \pmod 7$.
    
3. We need to find $2^{100}$. We can express the exponent 100 in terms of multiples of 6:
    
    $$100 = (16 \times 6) + 4$$
    
4. Therefore:
    
    $$2^{100} = 2^{16 \times 6} \cdot 2^4$$
    
    $$2^{100} = (2^6)^{16} \cdot 2^4$$
    
5. Substitute $2^6 \equiv 1$:
    
    $$2^{100} \equiv (1)^{16} \cdot 16 \pmod 7$$
    
    $$2^{100} \equiv 16 \pmod 7$$
    
6. $16 \div 7 = 2$ with a remainder of 2.
    

**Answer:** $2$

### Q2. Calculate $10^{100} \pmod{70}$. Why the previous calculation is different from calculation of $2^{100} \pmod 7$.

Solution:

Calculation:

We can separate the modulus $70$ into its prime factors: $7 \times 10$. Let $x = 10^{100}$.

1. **Mod 10:** $10^{100} \equiv 0 \pmod{10}$ (obviously, since it is a multiple of 10).
    
2. Mod 7: $10 \equiv 3 \pmod 7$. So, $10^{100} \equiv 3^{100} \pmod 7$.
    
    Using Fermat's Little Theorem ($3^6 \equiv 1 \pmod 7$):
    
    $$100 = 16 \times 6 + 4$$
    
    $$3^{100} \equiv 3^4 \pmod 7$$
    
    $$3^4 = 81$$
    
    $$81 \equiv 4 \pmod 7$$
    
3. Combine using CRT logic: We are looking for a number $x$ such that:
    
    $$x \equiv 0 \pmod{10}$$
    
    $$x \equiv 4 \pmod 7$$
    
    Multiples of 10 are: 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60...
    
    Check Mod 7:
    
    $10 \equiv 3$, $20 \equiv 6$, $30 \equiv 2$, $40 \equiv 5$, $50 \equiv 1$, $60 \equiv 4$.
    
    Therefore, $x = 60$.
    

**Answer:** $60$

Difference:

The calculation in Q1 ($2^{100} \pmod 7$) involves a prime modulus where $\gcd(2,7)=1$. This allows the direct application of Fermat's Little Theorem. In Q2 ($10^{100} \pmod{70}$), the modulus is composite, and the base (10) and modulus (70) are not coprime ($\gcd(10, 70) \neq 1$). Standard Euler's theorem cannot be applied directly without breaking the modulus down into factors.

### Q3. Define and discuss Euler Totient Function. Why it is important in cryptography.

Definition:

The Euler Totient Function, denoted as $\phi(n)$, counts the number of positive integers less than $n$ that are relatively prime (coprime) to $n$.

- If $p$ is prime, $\phi(p) = p - 1$.
    
- If $n = p \times q$ (where $p, q$ are primes), $\phi(n) = (p-1)(q-1)$.
    

**Importance in Cryptography:**

1. **RSA Algorithm:** It is the foundation of RSA key generation. The public exponent $e$ and private exponent $d$ are related by $e \cdot d \equiv 1 \pmod{\phi(n)}$. Without $\phi(n)$, we cannot compute the private decryption key.
    
2. **Euler's Theorem:** It generalizes Fermat's Little Theorem ($a^{\phi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod n$), which is essential for modular exponentiation used in encryption and decryption processes.

### Q4. Discuss why for Euler totient function $f(xy)$ following relation is true = $f(xy)=f(x)f(y)$ for integer $x,y>0$.

Discussion:

The relation $\phi(xy) = \phi(x)\phi(y)$ is only true if $x$ and $y$ are coprime (i.e., $\gcd(x, y) = 1$). This is known as the Multiplicative Property.

Reasoning:

If $x$ and $y$ share no common factors, the Chinese Remainder Theorem guarantees that for every pair of remainders $(a \pmod x, b \pmod y)$ where $\gcd(a,x)=1$ and $\gcd(b,y)=1$, there is a unique integer $z \pmod{xy}$ such that $\gcd(z, xy)=1$.

Essentially, the number of units in the ring $\mathbb{Z}_{xy}$ is equal to the product of the number of units in $\mathbb{Z}_x$ and $\mathbb{Z}_y$.

If $x$ and $y$ share a common factor, this relation fails (e.g., $\phi(2 \cdot 2) \neq \phi(2)\phi(2)$ because $2 \neq 1 \cdot 1$).

### Q5. Explain asymmetric key cryptography in a short note.

Asymmetric Key Cryptography (Public-Key Cryptography):

It is a cryptographic system that uses a pair of keys:

1. **Public Key:** Used for encryption and is shared openly.
    
2. **Private Key:** Used for decryption and is kept secret by the owner.
    

**Key Features:**

- **Authentication:** Using digital signatures, it proves the sender's identity (sender signs with Private, receiver verifies with Public).
    
- **Key Exchange:** Solves the problem of sharing secret keys over insecure channels (e.g., Diffie-Hellman).
    
- **Example Algorithms:** RSA, Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECC), Diffie-Hellman.
    
- **Trade-off:** It is computationally slower than symmetric cryptography, so it is often used to encrypt small session keys rather than large data files.

### Q6. Describe the differences between block and stream cipher. Explain with example.

|**Feature**|**Block Cipher**|**Stream Cipher**|
|---|---|---|
|**Operation**|Encrypts a fixed-size block of plaintext (e.g., 64 or 128 bits) at a time.|Encrypts plaintext one bit or one byte at a time.|
|**Complexity**|More complex hardware/software implementation.|Simple and faster to implement.|
|**Speed**|Generally slower due to processing blocks.|Generally faster.|
|**Error Propagation**|A bit error in ciphertext may scramble the entire block (depending on the mode).|A bit error in ciphertext affects only the corresponding bit in plaintext.|
|**Padding**|Requires padding if the message size isn't a multiple of the block size.|No padding required.|
|**Examples**|AES, DES, 3DES, Blowfish.|RC4, A5/1 (used in GSM), Salsa20.|

### Q7. Describe zero knowledge proof.

Definition:

A Zero-Knowledge Proof (ZKP) is a cryptographic protocol where one party (the Prover) proves to another party (the Verifier) that they know a specific value (like a secret password or key) without conveying any information apart from the fact that they know the value.

**Three Properties:**

1. **Completeness:** If the statement is true, an honest verifier will be convinced by an honest prover.
    
2. **Soundness:** If the statement is false, a dishonest prover cannot convince the verifier (except with negligible probability).
    
3. **Zero-Knowledge:** If the statement is true, the verifier learns nothing other than the fact that the statement is true.
    

Example (Ali Baba's Cave):

Peggy (Prover) wants to prove to Victor (Verifier) she has the magic word to open a door in a ring-shaped cave without revealing the word.

1. Peggy enters the cave and chooses path A or B randomly.
    
2. Victor waits outside, then shouts asking Peggy to come out via path A (or B).
    
3. If Peggy knows the secret word, she can open the connecting door and exit via whichever path Victor asks.
    
4. If they repeat this many times, the probability of Peggy "guessing" right without the password becomes infinitesimally small.

### Q8. Explain why cracking Diffie-Hellman key exchange is considered to be hard?

Cracking Diffie-Hellman is hard because it relies on the **Discrete Logarithm Problem (DLP)**.

The Math:

In Diffie-Hellman, we have public parameters $g$ (generator) and $p$ (prime).

Alice sends $A = g^a \pmod p$.

Bob sends $B = g^b \pmod p$.

To crack the key, an attacker (Eve) needs to find the private secret $a$ given only $A$, $g$, and $p$.

Equation: $a = \log_g(A) \pmod p$.

Why it is hard:

While computing exponentiation ($g^a$) is computationally fast (easy), performing the reverse operation (finding $a$ from the result) in modular arithmetic is extremely difficult for large prime numbers. There is no known efficient classical algorithm to solve the DLP in polynomial time. As $p$ increases in size (e.g., 2048 bits), the time required to brute force or solve this exceeds the age of the universe using current classical computers.

### Q9. Explain the reason behind the following belief: "With quantum computer, message encrypted with Diffie-Hellman algorithm can be cracked."

The belief is based on **Shor's Algorithm**.

1. **Classical Limit:** As stated in Q8, classical computers struggle with the Discrete Logarithm Problem because the best known algorithms (like Index Calculus or Number Field Sieve) are sub-exponential but still too slow for large keys.
    
2. **Quantum Capability:** Peter Shor developed a quantum algorithm (Shor's Algorithm) that can solve both the Integer Factorization problem (breaking RSA) and the Discrete Logarithm Problem (breaking Diffie-Hellman and ECC).
    
3. **Polynomial Time:** Shor's algorithm runs in polynomial time. This means if a sufficiently powerful quantum computer (with enough stable qubits) is built, it can calculate the private key from the public key almost as fast as the encryption was generated.
    
4. **Implication:** This renders the security assumptions of standard Diffie-Hellman null and void.

### Q10. Explain Diffie-Hellman algorithm with an example.

Overview:

The Diffie-Hellman (DH) Key Exchange allows two parties to establish a shared secret over an insecure channel. They do not exchange the secret key itself; they derive it.

**The Algorithm Steps:**

1. **Global Public Elements:** Both parties agree on a large prime number $p$ and a primitive root $g$.
    
2. **Key Generation (Alice):**
    
    - Selects a private random integer $a$ where $a < p$.
        
    - Calculates public key $A = g^a \pmod p$.
        
    - Sends $A$ to Bob.
        
3. **Key Generation (Bob):**
    
    - Selects a private random integer $b$ where $b < p$.
        
    - Calculates public key $B = g^b \pmod p$.
        
    - Sends $B$ to Alice.
        
4. **Secret Calculation:**
    
    - **Alice** computes secret $s = B^a \pmod p$.
        
    - **Bob** computes secret $s = A^b \pmod p$.
        
    - Both result in the same value because $(g^b)^a = (g^a)^b = g^{ab} \pmod p$.
        

Example:

Let $p = 23$ and $g = 5$.

1. **Alice:**
    
    - Chooses private key $a = 6$.
        
    - Calculates Public $A = 5^6 \pmod{23}$.
        
    - $5^2 = 25 \equiv 2$.
        
    - $5^6 = (5^2)^3 = 2^3 = 8$.
        
    - Alice sends **8** to Bob.
        
2. **Bob:**
    
    - Chooses private key $b = 15$.
        
    - Calculates Public $B = 5^{15} \pmod{23}$.
        
    - $5^{15} = (5^5)^3$. $5^2=2, 5^3=10, 5^5 = 10 \times 2 = 20 \equiv -3$.
        
    - $(-3)^3 = -27 \equiv 19 \pmod{23}$.
        
    - Bob sends **19** to Alice.
        
3. **Shared Secret:**
    
    - Alice computes: $s = 19^6 \pmod{23}$.
        
        $19 \equiv -4$. $(-4)^6 = 4096$.
        
        $4096 \pmod{23} = 2$.
        
    - Bob computes: $s = 8^{15} \pmod{23}$.
        
        $8^{15} = (2^3)^{15} = 2^{45} = 2^{22} \cdot 2^{23}$.
        
        Using Fermat's Little Theorem ($2^{22} \equiv 1$), $s = 1 \cdot 2^1 = 2$.
        

**Shared Secret Key is 2.**

### Q11. Describe different kinds of viruses and malwares.

**Malware (Malicious Software):** A broad term for any software designed to cause damage to a computer, server, or computer network.

**Types of Malware:**

1. **Virus:**
    
    - **Description:** A piece of code that inserts itself into another program (host). It cannot run independently.
        
    - **Behavior:** It replicates when the user executes the infected host program. It typically corrupts files or destroys data.
        
2. **Worm:**
    
    - **Description:** A standalone malware program that replicates itself to spread to other computers.
        
    - **Behavior:** Unlike a virus, it does not need to attach to an existing program or require user intervention (like opening a file) to spread. It exploits network vulnerabilities to consume bandwidth.
        
3. **Trojan Horse:**
    
    - **Description:** Disguises itself as legitimate or useful software.
        
    - **Behavior:** Users are tricked into loading and executing it on their systems. Once activated, it can enable cyber-criminals to spy on you, steal sensitive data, and gain backdoor access to your system. It does not replicate itself.
        
4. **Ransomware:**
    
    - **Description:** Malware that denies access to a computer system or data until a ransom is paid.
        
    - **Behavior:** It usually encrypts the victim's files with a strong cryptographic algorithm. The attacker holds the decryption key. (e.g., WannaCry).
        
5. **Spyware:**
    
    - **Description:** Software that aims to gather information about a person or organization without their knowledge.
        
    - **Behavior:** Monitors keystrokes (keyloggers), browsing habits, and login credentials to send to a third party.
        
6. **Rootkit:**
    
    - **Description:** A collection of tools that enable administrator-level access to a computer or network.
        
    - **Behavior:** It hides deep within the OS to conceal other malware or unauthorized access, making it very difficult to detect by standard antivirus software.
        
7. **Adware:**
    
    - **Description:** Software that automatically displays or downloads advertising material (often banners or pop-ups) when a user is online.
        
    - **Behavior:** While not always malicious, it can be aggressive and degrade system performance.
