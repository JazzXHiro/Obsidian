# Finite Difference Operators

# Numerical Operator and Their Relation

## $1.$ Forward Difference Operator:

Let $y = f(x)$ be a function where the argument $x$ can take the equidistant values  
$$a,\ a+h,\ a+2h,\ \dots,\ a+nh$$  
and $f(a),\ f(a+h),\ \dots,\ f(a+nh)$ are values of the given points.


> [!NOTE] Note
> h is the step size here.
> $\Delta \text{ is refered to as del.}$



---

### Definition:

The **forward difference operator** is defined as:

$$
\Delta f(x) = f(x + h) - f(x)
$$

---

### Higher Order Differences:

The **second order forward difference** is given by:

$$
\Delta^2 f(x) = \Delta\left[f(x + h) - f(x)\right] = f(x + 2h) - 2f(x + h) + f(x)
$$

---

### Examples:

- $\Delta f(x) = f(x + h) - f(x)$
- $\Delta f(x + h) = f(x + 2h) - f(x + h)$
- $\Delta f(x + 2h) = f(x + 3h) - f(x + 2h)$

---

### In General:

$$
\Delta f(x) = f(x + h) - f(x)
$$

$$
\Delta^2 f(x) = \Delta f(x + h) - \Delta f(x)
$$

Which simplifies to:

$$
\Delta^2 f(x) = f(x + 2h) - 2f(x + h) + f(x)
$$


---
# Finite Difference Operator – Important Notes

> 💡 **Note**:  
> $\Delta^2$ is **not** the square of the operator $\Delta$.  
> It means $\Delta$ operated by $\Delta$:
> $$
\Delta^2 = \Delta(\Delta)
$$

---
## Fundamental Theorem of Finite Differences

If  
$$
f(x) = a_0x^n + a_1x^{n-1} + a_2x^{n-2} + \dots + a_n
$$  
is an $n$-degree polynomial and $a_0 \neq 0$, then:
$$
\Delta^n f(x) = a_0 \cdot n! \cdot h^n
$$

---

## Example

Evaluate:
$$
\Delta^6 \left[(ax - 1)(bx^2 - 1)(cx^3 - 1)\right], \quad \text{where } h = 1
$$

### Solution:

First, expand the expression:
$$
\Delta^6 \left[ abcx^6 - bcx^5 - acx^4 + (ab + c)x^3 + bx^2 + ax - 1 \right]
$$

Now apply the rule:
$$
\Delta^6 f(x) = abc \cdot 6! \cdot h^6
$$

Since $h = 1$:
$$
= abc \cdot 720
$$

---

## Special Case:

If the degree of the polynomial is less than the order of the difference operator, the result is:
$$
\Delta^n f(x) = 0
$$  
For example:
$$
\Delta^2(x) = 0
$$  
since $x$ is a first-degree polynomial and $\Delta^2$ is a second-order operator.

---

# Finite Differences – Example Expansion

> 💡 **Given**:
> $$
\Delta^6 \left[(ax - 1)(bx^2 - 1)(cx^3 - 1)\right], \quad \text{where } h = 1
$$

---

## Step-by-step Expansion:

First expand the inner expression:
$$
(bx^2 - 1)(cx^3 - 1) = bcx^5 - bx^2 - cx^3 + 1
$$

Then multiply with $(ax - 1)$:
$$
(ax - 1)(bcx^5 - bx^2 - cx^3 + 1)
= abcx^6 - acx^4 - abx^3 + ax - bcx^5 + bx^2 + cx^3 - 1
$$

Rearranging:
$$
= abcx^6 - bcx^5 - acx^4 + (ab + c)x^3 + bx^2 + ax - 1
$$

---

## Applying Finite Difference Operator:

Since the polynomial is of degree 6, apply:
$$
\Delta^6 f(x) = a_0 \cdot 6! \cdot h^6
$$

Here, $a_0 = abc$ and $h = 1$, so:
$$
\Delta^6 f(x) = abc \cdot 720
$$

Hence:
$$
\Delta^6 \left[(ax - 1)(bx^2 - 1)(cx^3 - 1)\right] = abc \cdot 720
$$

# 🔁 Backward Difference Operator

## Definition

The backward difference operator is denoted by $\nabla \text{ refered to as Nebula}$.

If $f(x)$ is a function, then:

$$
\nabla f(x) = f(x) - f(x - h)
$$

## Second Backward Difference

$$
\nabla^2 f(x) = \nabla[\nabla f(x)]
= \nabla[f(x) - f(x - h)]
= \nabla f(x) - \nabla f(x - h)
$$

Now compute:

$$
= \left(f(x) - f(x - h)\right) - \left(f(x - h) - f(x - 2h)\right)
= f(x) - 2f(x - h) + f(x - 2h)
$$

---

## Example

Let:

$$
\nabla \tan x, \quad \text{where } h = 1
$$

### Solution:

$$
\nabla \tan x = \tan x - \tan(x - 1)
$$

Using the identity:

$$
\tan x = \frac{\sin x}{\cos x}, \quad \tan(x - 1) = \frac{\sin(x - 1)}{\cos(x - 1)}
$$

So:

$$
\nabla \tan x = \frac{\sin x}{\cos x} - \frac{\sin(x - 1)}{\cos(x - 1)}
$$

Find common denominator:

$$
= \frac{\sin x \cos(x - 1) - \cos x \sin(x - 1)}{\cos x \cos(x - 1)}
$$

Apply identity:

$$
= \frac{\sin(x - (x - 1))}{\cos x \cos(x - 1)} = \frac{\sin(1)}{\cos x \cos(x - 1)}
$$

---

## Summary:

- Forward Difference: $\Delta f(x) = f(x + h) - f(x)$  
- Backward Difference: $\nabla f(x) = f(x) - f(x - h)$

---

### 3. Identity Operator

The operator $I$ defined by $I(f(x)) = f(x)$ is known as the identity operator.

---

### 4. The Shifting Operator $E$

The operator $E$ defined by:

$$
E(f(x)) = f(x + h)
$$

is called the **shifting operator**.

Similar:

$$
E^{-1}(f(x)) = f(x - h)
$$

In general:

$$
E^n(f(x)) = f(x + nh), \quad E^{-n}(f(x)) = f(x - nh)
$$

We know that:

$$
\Delta f(x) = f(x + h) - f(x) = E(f(x)) - f(x)
$$

Hence:

$$
\Delta = E - 1
$$

And:

$$
\nabla f(x) = f(x) - f(x - h) = f(x) - E^{-1}(f(x))
$$

So:

$$
\nabla = 1 - E^{-1}
$$
