# Vectors & Linear Combinations

### **What is a Vector?**

A **vector** is a mathematical object that has both **magnitude** and **direction**. In linear algebra, vectors are represented as ordered lists of numbers (components) in a coordinate system.

For example, in **2D space**:

$$v = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$$

represents a vector with components 3 in the x-direction and 4 in the y-direction.

In **3D space**:

$$w = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ -2 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix}$$

has components along x, y, and z axes.

In general, an **nn-dimensional vector** is written as:

$$v = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \\ \vdots \\ v_n \end{bmatrix}$$

where each $v_i$ is a real number.

### **Vector Operations**

1. **Addition**: $$\begin{bmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} b_1 \\ b_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} a_1 + b_1 \\ a_2 + b_2 \end{bmatrix}$$
2. **Scalar Multiplication**: $$c\cdot \begin{bmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} c \cdot a_1 \\ c \cdot a_2 \end{bmatrix}$$

---

## **Linear Combinations of Vectors**

A **linear combination** of vectors involves multiplying each vector by a scalar and then adding the results.

For vectors $v_1, v_2, ..., v_n$ in $\mathbb{R}^m$, a linear combination is:

$$c_1 v_1 + c_2 v_2 + \dots + c_k v_k$$

where $c_1, c_2, ..., c_k$ are **scalars** (real numbers).

### **Example of a Linear Combination**

Let’s consider the vectors:

$$v_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad v_2 = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$$

A linear combination using scalars $2$ and $-4$ would be:

$$2 \cdot v_1 + (-4) \cdot v_2 = 2 \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} + (-4) \cdot \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}$$$$=\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} -12 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix}$$$$= \begin{bmatrix} -10 \\ 8 \end{bmatrix}$$

Thus, $\begin{bmatrix} -10 \\ 8 \end{bmatrix}$ is a **linear combination** of $v_1$ and $v_2$.

---

## **Linear Span and Basis**

- The **span** of a set of vectors $v_1, v_2, ..., v_k$ is the set of **all possible linear combinations** of those vectors.
- If the span of a set of vectors forms the entire space $\mathbb{R}^n$, those vectors are called a **basis** for $\mathbb{R}^n$.
