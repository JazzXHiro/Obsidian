# Scan Conversion - lines, circles and Ellipse

## Line-Drawing Algorithms

A straight-line segment in a scene is defined by the coordinate positions for the
endpoints of the segment. To display the line on a raster monitor, the graphics system must first project the endpoints to integer screen coordinates and determine the nearest pixel positions along the line path between the two end points. Then the line color is loaded into the frame buffer at the corresponding pixel coordinates.

Reading from the frame buffer, the video controller plots the screen pixels. This process digitizes the line into a set of discrete integer positions that, in general, only approximates the actual line path. Acomputed line position of (10.48, 20.51), for example, is converted to pixel position (10, 21). This rounding of coordinate values to integers causes all but horizontal and vertical lines to be displayed with a stair-step appearance (known as “the jaggies”)

#### Line Equations

The cartesian slope-intercept equation for a straight line -

$$y=mx+b$$

with $m$ as the slope of the line and $b$ as the $y$ intercept. Given that two end points of a line segment are specified at positions $(x_{0}, y_{{0}})$ and $(x_{end}, y_{end}).$
$$\begin{align}
m=\left( \frac{{y_{end} - y_{0}}}{x_{end} - x_{0}} \right) \\ \\
b = y_{0} - m.x_{0}
\end{align}$$

### Digital Differential Algorithm (DDA)

The Digital Differential Algorithm is used to draw a straight line between two given points in computer graphics.

> It works by calculating intermediate points using the slope of the line.

**Given:**

Let the endpoints be:

$$
\begin{align}
(x_1, y_1) \quad \text{and} \quad (x_2, y_2) \\
\end{align}
$$ 
$$
\begin{align}
\Delta x = x_2 - x_1 \\
\Delta y = y_2 - y_1  
\end{align}
$$

**Slope:**

$$
m = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}
$$

#### Algorithm Logic -

**Case 1: |m| < 1 (Line is less steep)**

Increment $x$ by $1$ and compute corresponding $y$.

$$\begin{align}
x_{i+1} = x_{i} + 1 \\
y_{i+1} = y_{i} + m
\end{align}$$


> [!NOTE] Why the + 1 in $x_{i}$ in above equation
> Because pixels exist at integer grid positions.
> 
> On the screen
> - We can not draw at $x=2.3$
> - We can not draw at $x=4.87$
>   
>   We can only draw at :
> $$(2,3),(3,3),(4,3)\dots$$
> 
> So we move one pixel at a time.
> 
> If slope is small:
> $$|m| < 1$$
> That means:
> $$Δx>Δy$$
> So the lines move more horizontally than vertically.
> 
> Therefore:
> - We step forward one pixel in x each iteration
> - Then calculate corresponding y

**Case 2: |m| $\geq$ 1 (Line is steep)**

Increment $y$ by $1$ and compute corresponding $x.$

$$\begin{align}
y_{i+1} = y_{i} + 1 \\
x_{i+1}=x_{i}+\frac{1}{m}
\end{align}$$

#### Steps of DDA Algorithm

1. Input $x_{1},y_{1},x_{2},y_{2}$
2. Compute:
	    $\Delta x=x2−x1$
	    $\Delta y=y_{2} - y_{1}$
3. Calculate number of steps:
$$steps=max(|\Delta x|,|\Delta y|)$$
4. Compute increments:
	   $$\begin{align}
	x_{inc}= \frac{\Delta x}{steps} \\
	y_{inc}=\frac{\Delta y}{steps}
	\end{align}$$
5. Start from $(x_{1},y_{1}).$
6. Plot next point using:
	   $$\begin{align}
		x=x+x_{inc} \\
		y=y+{y_{inc}}
		\end{align}$$
#### Example

1.
$$
\begin{align}
(x_1, y_1) = (1,1) \\
(x_2, y_2) = (7,4)  
\end{align}
$$
Calculate $\Delta x \ \text{and} \ \Delta y,$

$$\Delta x = x_2 - x_1 = 7 - 1 = 6$$
$$\Delta y = y_2 - y_1 = 4 - 1 = 3$$

Calculate Slope,

$$
\begin{align}
m = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} \\
m = \frac{3}{6} = 0.5
\end{align}
$$


Since:

$$|m| < 1$$
The line is **shallow**  
We increment **x by 1** each step.

For (|m| < 1):

$$x_{i+1} = x_i + 1$$
$$y_{i+1} = y_i + m$$$$m = 0.5$$

So:

$$y_{i+1} = y_i + 0.5$$
##### Iteration Table

| Step | x   | y (calculated) |
| ---- | --- | -------------- |
| 1    | 1   | 1              |
| 2    | 2   | 1 + 0.5 = 1.5  |
| 3    | 3   | 1.5 + 0.5 = 2  |
| 4    | 4   | 2 + 0.5 = 2.5  |
| 5    | 5   | 2.5 + 0.5 = 3  |
| 6    | 6   | 3 + 0.5 = 3.5  |
| 7    | 7   | 3.5 + 0.5 = 4  |

##### Points Generated (Before Rounding)

(1,1), (2,1.5), (3,2), (4,2.5), (5,3), (6,3.5), (7,4)

---

##### Pixel Points (After Rounding)

Since pixels must be integers, round y values:

(1,1), (2,2), (3,2), (4,3), (5,3), (6,4), (7,4)

---

##### Why This Works

Because:

$$
\begin{align}
m = \frac{y_{i+1} - y_i}{x_{i+1} - x_i} \\ \\
\text{Since, }x_{i+1} = x_{i} + 1   \\ \\
m = \frac{y_{i+1} - y_i}{x_{i}+1-x_{i}} \\ \\
m = \frac{y_{i+1} - y_i}{1} \\ \\
y_{i+1} - y_i = m   \\ \\
y_{i+1} = y_i + m
\end{align}
$$

#### Limitations of DDA

- Uses floating point arithmetic
- Slower compared to Bresenham's algorithm.
- Rounding errors may occur.

### Bresenham's Line Drawing Algorithm

#### Overview
Bresenham's algorithm is an efficient line drawing algorithm that uses integer arithmetic only, making it faster than DDA.

##### Given
Endpoints:
$$
\begin{align}
(x_{1}, y_{1}, (x_{2},y_{2})) \\ \\
\Delta x=x_{2}-x_{1} \\
\Delta y=y_{2}-y_{1}
\end{align}
$$
Slope:
$$
\begin{align}
m=\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}
\end{align}
$$
##### Decision Parameter
Initial decision parameter:
$$
\begin{align}
P_{0}=2\Delta y-\Delta x
\end{align}
$$

###### Case 1: m < 1
**If $P_{k}<0$**
$$
\begin{align}
x_{k+1} &=x_{k}+1 \\
y_{k+1}&=y_{k} \\
P_{k+1}&=P_{k}+2\Delta y
\end{align}
$$
**If $P_{k}\geq0$
$$
\begin{align}
x_{k+1} &=x_{k}+1 \\
y_{k+1}&=y_{k} +1 \\
P_{k+1}&=P_{k}+2\Delta y - 2\Delta x
\end{align}
$$

###### Case 2: m > 1
**If $P_{k}<0$
$$
\begin{align}
x_{k+1} &=x_{k} \\
y_{k+1}&=y_{k} + 1\\
P_{k+1}&=P_{k}+2\Delta x
\end{align}
$$
If $P_{k}\geq0$
$$
\begin{align}
x_{k+1} &=x_{k}+1 \\
y_{k+1}&=y_{k} +1 \\
P_{k+1}&=P_{k}+2\Delta x - 2\Delta y
\end{align}
$$

#### Steps of Bresenham's Algorithm

1. Input endpoints.

2. Compute Δx\Delta xΔx and Δy\Delta yΔy.

3. Initialize:
	$$P_{0}=2Δy−Δx$$
4. Repeat until x=x2x = x_2x=x2​:
    
    - If $Pk<0$ → choose East pixel
        
    - Else → choose North-East pixel
    
5. Update decision parameter accordingly.

- Faster than DDA.
    
- More accurate.

## Circle-Generating Algorithms

### Properties of circles

**Polynomial form of a circle centered at origin -**
$$x^2+y^2=r^2$$

A circle is defined as the set of points that are all at a given distance $r$ from a center position $(x_{c} , y_{c} )$. For any circle point $(x, y)$, this distance relationship is expressed by the Pythagorean theorem in Cartesian coordinates as
$$(x − x_{c})^2 + (y − y_{c})^2 = r^2$$
We could use this equation to calculate the position of points on a circle circumference by stepping along the x axis in unit steps from $x_{c}-r$ to $x_{c}+r$ and calculating the corresponding y values at each position as:

$$y=y_{c}\pm \sqrt{r^2-(x_{c}-x)^2}$$

Above isn't a good approach to generate a circle, it requires a considerable amount of computation at each step and doesn't output a uniform result in terms of spacing.

A way to eliminate this spacing issue is to calculate points along the circular boundary using polar coordinates $r$ and $\theta$.
$$\begin{align}
x=x_{c}+r\cos \theta \\
y=y_{c}+r\sin \theta
\end{align}$$
The amount of calculation done in above process is still inefficient.

#### 8-Way Symmetry

The slope of the curve in this octant has a magnitude less than or equal to 1.0. At x = 0, the circle slope is 0, and at x = y, the slope is −1.0.

##### 2. Trigonometric Method

**🔹 Parametric Representation**

A circle can be represented as:

$$\begin{align}
x=x_{c}+r\cos \theta \\
y=y_{c}+r\sin \theta
\end{align}$$

Where:

$$0 \leq \theta \leq 2\pi$$🔹 **Algorithm Idea**

1. Choose small angle increment $\Delta \theta$
    
2. For each $\theta$:
    
    - Compute $x$ and $y$
        
    - Plot point


This is still inefficient because to find the initial x and y we would require multiplication and square root operation from the above equations. A more efficient algorithm would be based on incremental calculation of decision parameters, as in the Bresenham line algorithm, which involves only simple integer operations.

Bresenham’s line algorithm for raster displays is adapted to circle generation by setting up decision parameters for finding the closest pixel to the circumference at each sampling step. The circle equation (from above), however, is nonlinear, so that square-root evaluations would be required to compute pixel distances from a circular path. Bresenham’s circle algorithm avoids these square-root calculations by comparing the squares of the pixel separation distances.

However, it is possible to perform a direct distance comparison without a squaring operation. The basic idea in this approach is to test the halfway position between two pixels to determine if this midpoint is inside or outside the circle boundary. This method is applied more easily to other conics; and for an integer circle radius, the midpoint approach generates the same pixel positions as the Bresenham circle algorithm. For a straight-line segment, the midpoint method is equivalent to the Bresenham line algorithm. Also, the error involved in locating pixel positions along any conic section using the midpoint test is limited to half the pixel separation.
